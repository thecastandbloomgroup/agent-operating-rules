## Headline

Stop running retrospectives as feedback collections; run each one as a meeting that ends with one assigned, tested change. The plan has four steps:

1. Measure the failure point first, because the fix depends on where the process breaks.
2. Then enforce one output rule per session: one change, one owner, one due date, one success check.
3. In parallel, run the top change as a two-week, budget-capped probe against one metric.
4. After four weeks, score the new format with a rubric and keep or change it.

## Background

Our retrospectives happen, people attend, and no process change follows. The request is a plan to make them produce results.

## Relevance

A retrospective with no output costs about one hour per person per session and hides process problems [judgment]. Fixing the output rule therefore pays back within the first month [judgment].

## Information

Three causes appear in most non-productive retrospectives, in rough order of frequency [judgment]:

1. Actions are proposed but never assigned, so no one owns them.
2. Too many actions are chosen, so none get done.
3. Actions are too large to finish between sessions.

The recommendation assumes cause 1 and 2 dominate. See the assumption log below.

`Assumption: The team has authority to change its own process. Why not measured: the material does not state the approval path. Validate by: ask one team member who signs off. If wrong: add a step to secure authority from the process owner before Step 3.`
`Assumption: Follow-through, not discussion silence, is the failure point. Why not measured: the material contains no session data. Validate by: Step 1 below. If wrong: shift the plan to psychological safety work; see the Follow-up section.`

Alternative considered: replace retrospectives with an async survey or delete them. This loses because the survey keeps the same missing output rule and deletion removes the feedback loop [judgment].
