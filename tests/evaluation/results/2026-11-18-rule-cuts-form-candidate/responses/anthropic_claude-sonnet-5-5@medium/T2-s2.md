## Headline

No prompt stops hallucination fully, but a grounding prompt with a refusal rule reduces it, and you must measure the result.

- Hallucination means output that states false or unsupported facts. Prompts reduce it but do not remove it [1].
- Supplying source text and demanding quotes gives the model less room to invent [2].
- A refusal phrase gives the model a safe answer when the source is silent.
- A test set shows whether the prompt works on your task.

## Background

## Relevance

A prompt that sounds safe but is untested gives false confidence. Users then trust wrong answers.

## Information

Here is the prompt. Paste it into the system prompt, and put your documents between the source tags.

```text
Answer using only the text between <source> tags.
After each claim, quote the sentence that supports it.
If the source does not contain the answer, reply exactly: Not in the source.
Do not use outside knowledge.
Do not guess.
Do not invent names, numbers, dates, or links.
```

Evidence:
- Hallucination has several causes, including gaps in training data and the model's tendency to produce fluent text. A prompt addresses only some of them [1].
- Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) means the system fetches relevant passages and gives them to the model. It improved factual accuracy on knowledge tasks [2].
- It is very likely (90 to 100%) that some unsupported claims remain after the prompt change [judgment].
- The size of the reduction on your task is unknown until you run the test.
