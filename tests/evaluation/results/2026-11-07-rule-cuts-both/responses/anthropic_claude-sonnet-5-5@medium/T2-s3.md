## Headline

No prompt stops hallucination fully, but one grounded prompt cuts it the most: it limits the model to supplied sources, demands quotes, and allows "Not in the sources."

- The model produces fluent text, so it fills gaps unless the prompt removes the need to fill them.
- Supplied sources give the model facts to use instead of facts to recall.
- A quote requirement makes each claim checkable.
- An allowed "no answer" removes the pressure to guess.

## Background

Hallucination means output that sounds confident but is false or unsupported. You ask for one prompt that stops it. Research shows that grounding and verification reduce it, and that no single wording removes it [1][2].

## Relevance

A wrong answer that sounds sure costs more than a missing answer. A prompt that forces sources and quotes lets you catch errors before you act on them.

## Information

Use this prompt. Put your source text where marked.

```text
Answer only from the sources below.
Do not use outside knowledge. Do not guess.
For each claim, quote the exact sentence that supports it.
If the sources do not contain the answer, write: "Not in the sources."
At the end, list any claim that has no quote.

Sources:
<paste text here>

Question:
<your question>
```

Evidence:
- Retrieval of source text before generation improves factual accuracy on knowledge tasks, per Lewis et al. [1].
- A second pass that checks the draft against its own verification questions reduces false claims, per Dhuliawala et al. [2].
- Hallucination has several causes, including gaps in training data and the way models are trained to continue text [3].
- The exact size of the gain from this prompt depends on the model and task. [judgment]
