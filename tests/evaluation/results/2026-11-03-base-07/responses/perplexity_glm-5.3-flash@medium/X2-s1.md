## Headline

Stop treating retrospectives as meetings. Convert each one into a single committed change with an owner, a measure, and a check date.

- Retrospectives fail to change work because actions are generic, unowned, and never reviewed.
- A change that no one owns and no test dates gets dropped in a week.
- Meet less, decide one thing, and verify that this thing lands.
- Track the ratio of actions completed to actions agreed, because this ratio is the signal of process health.

## Background

The team holds retrospectives, but the outcomes never reach daily work [inference]. The request asks for a fix. The plan below assumes a standard iteration cadence and a collocated or remote team of five to nine people.

## Relevance

Retrospectives consume roughly one to two percent of team capacity per iteration [judgment]. That budget buys nothing today. A fix restores the team's main lever for improvement.

## Information

**Analysis.** A reviewer would ask why actions die. Three causes cover the field, and they do not overlap:

1. The action was never specific enough to assign.
2. The action had an owner but no time, because the backlog held priority.
3. The team never checked whether past actions landed, because the next retro started from a blank page [3], [18].

Cause 1 is a format problem. Causes 2 and 3 are commitment problems. The themes repeat across widely reported retro failures: action lists grow, close rates trend to zero, and attendance follows [judgment]. Few published studies measure retro close rates, so this rests on practitioner reports and team observation, not controlled data.

**Assumption log.**

- A1. The team runs retrospectives on a regular cadence. Why not measured: the request does not state it. Validate by: ask the team lead in the next meeting. If wrong: add cadence setup as step zero.
- A2. Action items go into a document, not the backlog. Why not measured: the request does not state where actions live. Validate by: inspect the retro notes and the backlog. If wrong: step 1 changes from migration to policy.
- A3. Nothing blocks the team from changing its own process. Why not measured: measuring needs one observed change attempt, which costs a session. Validate by: run step 2 once. If wrong: escalate the blocker to the manager instead.

**Alternative considered.** Hire an external facilitator to run retros. It loses because facilitation alone does not create commitment, and the cost is not justified before a cheap internal fix is tried [judgment].

## End

I recommend that we make retrospectives produce exactly one committed change each, verified against a completion ratio.

1. **Move every retro action into the shared backlog with an owner and a due date.** This removes cause 1 and cause 2: nothing sits in a notes file. Increment: migrate the current open actions in one session. Test: deterministic, Clear, because the rule is known to everyone.
   ```gherkin
   Given a retro action agreed today
   When the team lead checks the backlog
   Then the action has an owner, a due date, and priority
   ```
2. **Start each retrospective with the completion ratio of the last one.** The ratio is actions closed divided by actions agreed. State the number first, then discuss why. This removes cause 3: review of the past actions becomes the entry point. Increment: add one slide or line to the next meeting. Test: deterministic, Clear, because the count is mechanical.
   ```gherkin
   Given the last retrospective agreed 4 actions
   When the next retrospective starts
   Then the facilitator reads the completion ratio aloud
   ```
3. **Run a two iteration probe: agree to one action per retro, capped at one hour of work.** Cap the probe at two iterations and two changes. If the ratio is below 50% after two iterations, stop and escalate the priority conflict to the manager. This tests cause 2 without a full process redesign. Increment: one retro cycle with the one action rule. Test: stochastic, Complex, because team response is only visible after the run; the completion ratio at 50% is the threshold, sample size is two iterations, and leverage is a guess.
4. **Interview each team member for ten minutes on why past actions died.** This classifies which of the three causes dominates for this team, so later steps target the real blocker. Increment: five interviews, or one survey, in one session. Test: pass means at least one named, repeated blocker appears in three of five interviews; baseline is today, where no causes are named. Kind: evaluative; rubric criteria are clarity of cause named, frequency across interviews, and evidence given, weights 0.4, 0.3, 0.3, pass threshold 70%. Cynefin: Complicated, because analysis of interviews picks the dominant cause. Step 4 MUST NOT constrain steps 1 and 2, because those run regardless. However, if step 4 finds a management level blocker, replace step 3 with an escalation step.

Steps 1 and 2 go first because they are Clear and they reduce the uncertainty of the step 3 probe. Step 4 runs in parallel with them.

## Follow-up

- Which team role currently keeps the retro notes and controls the backlog? The answer decides who owns step 1. I will assume the team lead owns both.
- If A1 is false and the cadence is irregular, fix the calendar before step 2, because a ratio needs a fixed interval to be comparable.
