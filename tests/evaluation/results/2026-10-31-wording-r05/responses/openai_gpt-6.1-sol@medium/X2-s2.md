## Headline

Replace open-ended retrospectives with one small change, a named owner, and a review of what actually happened.

- First, find where previous actions stop: agreement, execution, or review. [judgment]
- Then, test one change instead of creating another list. [judgment]
- Finally, separate team-controlled changes from problems that require outside authority. [judgment]

## Background

People attend your retrospectives, but you report no resulting change. [1] Attendance therefore does not tell you whether the meetings help.

## Relevance

The next retrospective should produce evidence of progress, not just another discussion. [judgment] A short trial can reveal whether the main barrier concerns follow-through or authority. [judgment]

## Information

**The purpose is change, not discussion.** The Agile Manifesto links regular reflection with changes in team behavior. [2]

The Scrum Guide likewise describes retrospectives as a way to plan improvements and address the most helpful changes promptly. [3]

**Check three distinct failure points:**

| Failure point | Evidence to check | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Agreement | No specific action, owner, or review date | Define one testable action. |
| Execution | An agreed action never starts or finishes | Allocate time or escalate its dependency. |
| Review | An action finishes, but nobody checks its effect | Compare the result with the expected result. |

This table is a diagnostic proposal, not a finding about your team. [judgment]

**Also check whether people can name the real problem.** Offer a private route to the facilitator before the next meeting. [judgment] Do not treat silence as agreement. [judgment]

## End

I recommend that we take these three steps. These tests describe proposed work, not completed results.

### 1. Trace the last three retrospectives

**Test first, deterministic:** Given the available records, when each action is checked, then its owner, status, review date, and dependency are visible.

Spend 20 minutes creating that record. Mark missing information explicitly rather than reconstructing agreement from memory.

This removes the blocker of not knowing where actions stop. [judgment]

**Domain: Clear**, because this step records facts against fixed fields.

### 2. Run a two-cycle trial with one change

**Test first, stochastic:**
- **Metric:** Changes completed with an observed useful result.
- **Baseline:** The count from Step 1, reported per retrospective.
- **Sample:** Two retrospective cycles.
- **Pass threshold:** At least one useful change, supported by an agreed measure or concrete observation.

After Step 1, select one team-controlled change. Record its owner, expected result, review date, and protected work time.

For example: “For one week, reserve ten minutes daily to resolve blocked work; compare blocked-item age before and afterward.”

Cap extra effort at one team-hour per cycle. Stop the trial if it causes harm or exceeds that budget.

This tests whether a smaller commitment produces action. [judgment]

**Domain: Complex**, because the effect depends on team behavior. This is a small probe; its expected benefit remains a guess.

### 3. Decide from the result, not from attendance

**Test first, deterministic:** Given the trial results, when the team reviews them, then every action receives one disposition: keep, revise, stop, or escalate.

After both cycles, hold a 15-minute review, whether the trial passes or fails.

Keep changes with useful results. Stop changes without useful results. Escalate unresolved dependencies to someone with authority, with a named request and response date.

This prevents unfinished actions from disappearing between meetings. [judgment]

**Domain: Clear**, because the team applies an explicit review rule.

## Follow-up

If people cannot safely name problems, prioritize a confidential route and leadership conduct before another group exercise. [judgment]

If outside dependencies dominate, replace further team experiments with a decision meeting involving the relevant owner. [judgment]
