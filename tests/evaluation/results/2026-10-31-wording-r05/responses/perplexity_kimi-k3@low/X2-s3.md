# Headline

Retrospectives fail when they produce discussion without decisions, so convert each one into one owned, tested change. Three mechanisms fix this: limit actions to one per retro, assign a single owner with a due date, and review last retro's action first. Close the loop on whether the change worked before adding another.

# Background

The team holds retrospectives but attendance does not turn into changed behavior. Nothing in the team's process shifts from one retro to the next.

# Relevance

A retro that produces no change trains people to disengage. That wastes one to two hours per sprint and erodes trust in the whole improvement process.

# Information

Common causes of stagnant retrospectives, drawn from agile practice literature [1][2][3], grouped by theme:

1. **Too many actions** (support: 3 sources). Teams list five to ten improvements and complete none.
2. **No owner or deadline** (support: 3 sources). "We should communicate better" has no person and no date.
3. **No follow-up** (support: 2 sources). The next retro never checks the last action, so nothing is accountable.
4. **Same format every time** (support: 2 sources, [judgment]). Repetition lowers energy and surfaces the same complaints.

Typical completion rates tell the story informally: teams that take one action per retro complete most of them, while teams that take three or more complete few [judgment, no source].

**Assumption log**

A1. The team runs retros regularly (every one to three weeks). Why not measured: the material does not state it. Validate by: ask the facilitator. If wrong: first establish a fixed cadence.

A2. The retro already captures some action items, they just stall. Why not measured: the material does not state it. Validate by: read the last three retro notes. If wrong: add a capture step before fixing ownership.

**Alternative considered**: replace retros with a different improvement forum (for example, a dedicated continuous-improvement meeting). It loses because the format is rarely the problem; unowned, untested actions are [judgment] [1]. Changing the meeting name without adding ownership reproduces the failure.

# End

I recommend that we do the following, in order:

1. **Open the next retro by reviewing the previous action items.** Blocker removed: no accountability loop. Test (deterministic): Given the retro agenda, When it starts, Then the first ten minutes check each prior action as done, not done, or dropped with a reason. Domain: Clear, because a known best practice exists. [1]
2. **Limit the retro to one action, with one named owner and a due date.** Blocker removed: too many unowned actions. Test (deterministic): Given the retro ends, When the notes are written, Then they contain exactly one action, one owner by name, and a date. Domain: Clear, because the practice is standard. [1][2]
3. **Run a two-sprint trial of this format as a capped probe.** Budget: two sprints. Stop condition: if both actions still stall, escalate to Step 4. Measure: action completion rate against the current baseline of roughly zero [judgment]. Domain: Complex, because team response is only visible afterward. Runs in parallel with Steps 1 and 2.
4. **If the probe fails, interview five team members one-on-one.** Blocker removed: unknown root cause behind the stall. Test (evaluative): rubric with three criteria (psychological safety, management follow-through, time pressure), weight safety at 50%, pass when at least one criterion is confirmed by three of five interviewees with quoted evidence. Domain: Complicated, because analysis of interview data picks the next move. Step 4 MUST NOT start until the probe in Step 3 ends.

If A1 is false, replace Step 1 with setting a fixed retro cadence first.
