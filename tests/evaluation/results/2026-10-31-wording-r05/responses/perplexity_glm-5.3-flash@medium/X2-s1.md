## Headline

Turn the retrospective into the first step of a change loop: key one decision per meeting, give it one owner, and open the next meeting by verifying it. Support:

- The failure is usually not the meeting; it is the missing loop from decision to shipped change [judgment].
- Log what happened to past action items first, because that data decides the rest of the plan.
- One owned item per cycle survives contact with real work; five unowned items do not [1].

## Background

The team runs retrospectives. People attend, discussion happens, and no process changes afterwards. The request is a fix for this pattern.

## Relevance

An unactioned retrospective costs one hour per person per cycle and returns the same complaints each time. The cost compounds every sprint, so the fix has leverage now.

## Information

Evidence

- Derby and Larsen state that a retrospective should close with two to three action items at most, each with a single owner and a date; a longer list goes undone [1].
- Edmondson shows that people hide real problems when raising them risks blame, so shallow retrospectives are a known safety symptom, not just a facilitation symptom [2].
- Snowden and Boone state that complex problems respond to small probes with a measure and a stop condition, not to plans agreed in discussion [3].

Thematic grouping of these sources shows one shared theme: "no follow-through loop", supported by all 3 sources. No source disagrees. The safety theme is a second possible cause [2]. Because the candidate causes differ in fix, the plan starts by measuring which cause applies here.

Assumptions

- A1. Past retrospectives produced written action items, and the items were not done. Why not measured: the request does not state it. Validate by: the audit in Step 1. If wrong: no items exist to verify, so the meeting needs a decision step, not a follow-through step.
- A2. The team can spare about two hours per sprint to complete one small change. Why not measured: the request does not state available capacity. Validate by: ask at the start of the next meeting. If wrong: shrink items to tasks inside existing tickets.
- A3. Attendees include people who hold the authority to change the process. Why not measured: the request does not state who attends. Validate by: one question in Step 1. If wrong: route the chosen item to a sponsor outside the meeting.

Alternative considered

Cancel retrospectives and run postmortems only after incidents. It loses because prevention needs a feedback cycle shorter than the incident cycle, and the meeting already exists at a fixed cadence, so the loop is cheap to repair [judgment].

## End

I recommend that we run the steps below. Step 1 comes first because its output decides the rest. Steps 3 and 4 MUST NOT start until Step 1 names the dominant cause.

1. **Classify the failure mode (Confused, because the cause is not yet known and it decides the fix).** Increment: a one-session audit of the last three retrospectives, tallying each action item as done, owned-not-done, or never owned. Blocker removed: no data on where items die. Test (written first, deterministic): Given the notes of the last three retrospectives, When the tallies run, Then every item has exactly one bucket and the row counts sum to the item count.
2. **Adopt the one-item rule (Clear, because a best practice exists and applying it is one step).** Increment: at the end of the next retrospective, the team picks exactly one item, names one owner, sets one date, and opens the next meeting by verifying it. Blocker removed: the full-list-no-owner pattern that leaves items undone [1]. Test (written first, deterministic): Given a retrospective has ended, When the actions are reviewed, Then one item exists with an owner, a date, and a ticket, and the next meeting starts with its status.
3. **Run a five-why analysis on the top theme before choosing the item (Complicated, because analysis by an expert picks the correct action from more than one good option).** Increment: one 30-minute analysis on the highest-voted repeated topic. Blocker removed: symptom-level fixes that let the same issue return each sprint. Test (written first, evaluative, Kepner-Tregoe style rubric): criteria are "removes recurrence" (weight 5), "fits one sprint" (weight 3), "measurable" (weight 2); pass threshold is 7 of 10 with at least 4 on "removes recurrence"; evidence is the audit tally from Step 1.
4. **Run one change probe per sprint in parallel with steps 2 and 3 (Complex, because cause and effect of a team process change show up only after the trial).** Increment: convert the chosen item into a probe with a metric, a baseline, a stop condition, and a budget of 2 hours; this probe MUST NOT consume more than that budget. Blocker removed: the team gains the capability to test changes cheaply instead of debating them [3]. Test (written first, stochastic): metric is the issue count in the theme, baseline is the count from the last three sprints, pass threshold is one visible reduction within two sprints, sample size is two sprint data points at minimum.

Hedge per Section 6.8: if the Step 1 audit shows A1 is false, and no items were ever written, replace Step 2 with a "write the decision" step first, because a verification loop without a written item has nothing to verify.

## Follow-up

Conditions that change the recommendation: a low survey or audit signal on blame, which would add an anonymous topic intake to Step 2, per Edmondson [2]. A2 false, which shrinks Step 4 to a task inside an existing ticket.
