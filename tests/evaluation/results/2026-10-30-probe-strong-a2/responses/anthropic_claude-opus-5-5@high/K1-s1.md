## Headline

Fix the 8-connection pool cap first, then restore the cache TTL (time to live).

- The pool cut from 50 to 8 connections per pod adds 687 ms of the 780 ms p95 (95th percentile) rise.
  - Spans: traces.txt, span table [1]
  - Pool size: release.diff, config/db.yaml [2]
- The TTL cut from 300 s to 60 s raises the cache miss rate from 12% to 31%.
  - TTL: release.diff, config/cache.yaml [2]
  - Miss rate: traces.txt, span table [1]
- The ORM (object-relational mapping) change adds no measurable latency, because each query span moved by 6 ms or less [1].

## Background

Release 2026.10.21 changed the ORM query, the cache TTL, and the database connection pool [2]. After it, p95 latency for GET /customers/{id}/orders rose from 120 ms to 900 ms [1]. The request asks for a plan to fix it.

## Relevance

The error rate rose from 0.02% to 0.40%, and 96% of the errors are pool timeouts [1]. The release has run in production for nine days, since 2026-10-21 14:05 UTC [2].

## Information

**Table 1.** p95 latency by span, window A (2026-10-20) and window B (2026-10-22), 10,000 sampled requests each [1].

| Span | A p95 ms | B p95 ms | Change ms |
|---|---|---|---|
| http.request (total) | 120 | 900 | +780 |
| db.pool.acquire | 3 | 690 | +687 |

**Finding 1: pool wait is the main cause.** Confidence: high, because three independent sources agree.

- Pool acquire p95 rose from 3 ms to 690 ms, while each query span moved by 6 ms or less [1].
- The release cut max_size from 50 to 8 per pod, and acquire_timeout_s from 30 s to 5 s [2].
- Logs show "pool exhausted" warnings on three of four pods within eight seconds [3].
- These warnings rose from none per hour on 2026-10-20 to 1,800 to 2,400 per hour on 2026-10-22 [3].
- Logs show PoolTimeout errors at 5 s, each with a 503 response of about 5,000 ms [3].
- Database server processor load (CPU) at peak moved from 38% to 41% [1].
- Therefore the database server has spare capacity, and the connection count is the limit [inference].

**Finding 2: demand is above 8 connections.**

- In window A, the service held 31 database connections at peak [1].
- In window B, the peak fell to 8 at almost the same traffic: 405 against 410 requests per second [1].
- Little's law states that the average number of items in a system equals the arrival rate times the time in the system [4].
- At 405 requests per second and 29 ms of query time, the average need is about 12 connections [inference].
- So a total cap of 8 is too small, if every request queries the database (see A1 and A3).

**Finding 3: the cache change adds database load.**

- The cache miss rate rose from 12% to 31% [1].
- At about 405 requests per second, misses rise from about 49 to 126 per second, that is, 2.6 times [inference].
- Each miss reads from the database (see A1).
- The cache.get span stayed at 4 to 5 ms p95, so the cache server is not slow [1].

**Finding 4: the ORM change has no measured cost.**

- The orders query p95 moved from 52 ms to 58 ms, and order_lines moved from 30 ms to 26 ms [1].
- The net change is 2 ms, so the ORM change needs no action [inference].
- Keep acquire_timeout_s at 5 s. A return to 30 s converts fast errors into waits of up to 30 s [inference].

**Conflicts in the evidence.**

- The traces report a database-side peak of 8 connections in window B [1].
- However, the logs report 8 of 8 connections in use on three pods within eight seconds [3].
- These facts fit a total cap of 8 only if pods take turns at the full cap [inference].
- Logs also show 11 and 14 waiters on single pods [3].
- Each pod runs 8 worker threads [2].
- So more callers wait for connections than threads exist, and the material does not explain the extra callers [inference].
- Step 1 in the End section resolves both conflicts.

**Assumption log.**

A1. A cache hit needs no database connection, and a miss needs one. Why not measured: the material does not state it. Validate by: read the cache path in the orders handler code. If wrong: Step 4 does not reduce pool demand, so drop it.

A2. The TTL cut to 60 s serves no data freshness need. Why not measured: the material does not state it, and cache.yaml has no commit message. Validate by: ask the author of the cache.yaml commit. If wrong: drop Step 4.

A3. Each request holds a connection for about its query time, 29 ms at p50 (median). Why not measured: no tool in this session can reach the pods. Validate by: add a span from pool acquire to pool release on one pod. If wrong: the connection need rises, and Step 2 needs a higher limit.

A4. OPS-412 sets a limit of 8 connections for the whole service, not for each pod. Why not measured: the material does not state it. Validate by: Step 1. If wrong: see Follow-up, condition 2.

**Alternative considered.**

A full revert to release 2026.10.14 restores the earlier 120 ms p95 in one deploy [1].

It loses because it allows up to 200 connections (4 pods times 50) against the OPS-412 limit [2].

The ORM change that it removes has no measured cost (see Finding 4).

## End

I recommend that we take these four steps. Steps 1 to 3 remove the 687 ms pool wait. Step 4 removes the smaller cache load.

**Step 1. Measure the real connection limit and the use per pod.**

- Increment: read ticket OPS-412. Then query orders-db for the service role limit and the open connections per pod at peak.
- Blocker removed: the logs and traces conflict on whether 8 is a cap per pod or a total cap.
- Kind: deterministic. Domain: Clear, because a database query reports the limit directly.
- Test, written first:

```gherkin
Given orders-db.internal during peak traffic
When we read the connection limit for the service role
And we count open connections and pool waiters on each pod
Then we record the limit L and four per-pod counts
And the four per-pod counts sum to the database-side count
```

**Step 2. Choose the capacity fix.**

- Increment: score three options in a Kepner-Tregoe decision analysis, that is, must-have criteria plus weighted wants [5].
  - Option a: raise the OPS-412 limit to at least 32 and keep max_size at 8 per pod.
  - Option b: add a connection pooler, that is, a proxy that shares a few server connections among many clients. PgBouncer in transaction mode is one example.
  - Option c: release each connection right after its last query. This option applies only if A3 is false.
- Blocker removed: no option can be chosen until L is known.
- Kind: evaluative. Domain: Complicated, because more than one good practice exists.
- Rubric, written first:

| Criterion | Type | Weight | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total server connections stay at or below L | Must | none | Step 1 result |
| Estimated acquire p95 at 410 requests per second is 10 ms or less | Want | 5 | Little's law with Step 1 data |
| The change fits in one working session | Want | 3 | Estimate from the service owner |
| A revert takes 10 minutes or less | Want | 2 | Deploy history |

- Score each want from 0 to 10 and multiply it by its weight. The maximum is 100.
- Pass threshold: the option meets the Must and scores 70 or more.
- Dependency: Step 2 MUST NOT start until Step 1 records L.

**Step 3. Deploy the chosen fix to one pod first.**

- Increment: apply the fix to api-1 as a canary (one pod that gets the change first). Compare it with the other three pods at peak.
- Blocker removed: the 687 ms pool wait.
- Kind: stochastic. Domain: Complicated, because the analysis in Step 2 picks the practice.
- Acceptance test, written first:
  - Metric: p95 of http.request and of db.pool.acquire for GET /customers/{id}/orders.
  - Baseline: window A, 120 ms total and 3 ms acquire (Table 1).
  - Pass threshold: total p95 of 150 ms or less, acquire p95 of 10 ms or less, and zero PoolTimeout errors [judgment].
  - Sample size: 10,000 sampled requests at peak on the canary pod.
  - Stop condition: revert the canary if its error rate exceeds 0.40% or its p95 exceeds 900 ms.
- Dependency: Step 3 MUST NOT start until the rubric in Step 2 passes. Extend the fix to all pods only after the canary passes.

**Step 4. Restore the cache TTL to 300 s.**

- Increment: change default_ttl_s from 60 to 300 in config/cache.yaml, and deploy it.
- Blocker removed: the 31% miss rate sends about 2.6 times more reads to the database.
- Kind: deterministic. Domain: Clear.
- Test, written first:

```gherkin
Given config/cache.yaml on the release branch
When the cache client loads its settings
Then default_ttl_s equals 300
And a new orders:customer key in Redis reports a TTL between 61 s and 300 s
```

- Dependency: Step 4 MAY start in parallel with Step 1, because it does not depend on L. Step 4 MUST NOT start until A2 is confirmed.
