# Headline

Retrospectives fail when they never reach a decision, an owner, and a deadline; fix those three gates.

1. End each retro with at most two actions that have one owner and a date.
2. Open each retro by reviewing last time's actions before new topics.
3. Rotate the facilitator so one voice does not control which issues die.

# Background

Your team still meets, but the meetings no longer produce change. This is a common pattern called retro theater: attendance stays, follow-through stops.

# Relevance

If this continues, people stop raising real problems, and trust in the process erodes. Restoring follow-through is cheaper than rebuilding trust later.

# Information

Evidence on why retros fail comes from agile practice literature and practitioner reports [1], [2]. Three failure modes appear most often:

- No commitment gate: discussions end without a decision. Three sources support this theme [1], [2], [3].
- No accountability: actions have no named owner or review. Two sources support this [1], [2].
- Safety or dominance problems: the same people speak, real issues stay hidden. Two sources support this [2], [3]. Confidence is moderate because the sources are secondary and no source measured your team directly.

Assumption log:

`A1. Your team uses a standard agile cadence of one to three weeks. Why not measured: the material does not state it. Validate by: ask the team lead. If wrong: adjust cadence advice, the three gates stay the same.`

Alternative considered: canceling retros entirely and switching to continuous feedback. It loses because mature teams report that a regular protected slot still surfaces problems that daily work hides [2], [judgment].

# End

I recommend that we:

1. Add a commitment gate to the next retro: end with at most two actions, one owner each, and a date. This removes the no-decision blocker. Evaluative. Rubric first: an action passes when it has an owner, a date, and a verb; all three criteria carry equal weight; pass threshold is 100% of adopted actions. Complicated domain.
2. Add a review step at the start of the next retro: check each prior action as done, dropped, or blocked. This closes the accountability loop. Deterministic. Test (Gherkin): Given the retro starts, When the facilitator opens, Then the first agenda item is the prior action review. Clear domain.
3. Rotate the facilitator each session for the next three sessions. This breaks one-voice dominance. Stochastic. Acceptance test first: measure count of distinct speakers per retro against baseline of last three retros; pass when at least 70% of attendees speak, sample size three sessions. Safe-to-fail probe in the Complex domain; stop if participation drops.
4. Run an anonymous check on safety before retro four. This classifies whether fear blocks honest topics. Stochastic. Acceptance test first: five-point safety survey, baseline none, pass threshold average of 3.5 or higher over 100% of attendees. Confused domain action, because its result decides the next step.

Step 4 SHOULD run in parallel with Steps 1 to 3. If safety scores below 3.5, replace open discussion with anonymous topic submission [2].

# Follow-up

Conditions that change the recommendation: a low safety score (move to Steps toward psychological safety first), or a team larger than twelve (split the retro).
