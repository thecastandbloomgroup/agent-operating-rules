Yes, turn on gzip (or Brotli, which typically compresses a further 15 to 20 percent smaller than gzip for JSON).

The reasons:

- JSON averages 400 KB per response, which is large for wire transfer. On a typical 10 Mbps connection, 400 KB takes roughly 320 ms of transfer time alone.
- JSON is repetitive text, so compression ratios of 70 to 90 percent are typical. Expect responses to drop to roughly 40 to 120 KB.
- Virtually all HTTP clients send Accept-Encoding: gzip and decompress transparently, so adoption cost is near zero.

Points to check:

1. CPU cost. Gzip at a moderate level (4 to 6) costs little; the transfer savings almost always outweigh it at 400 KB sizes. Avoid level 9, which burns CPU for marginal size gains.
2. Do not compress responses that are already compressed or tiny. Set a minimum threshold, commonly 1 KB. Your 400 KB responses are far above it.
3. HTTPS vs HTTP. Compression over HTTPS is fine for typical APIs. The BREACH attack only matters when responses mix attacker input with secrets and compress them together; most JSON APIs are safe.
4. Disable proxy or CDN pre-compression conflicts. If a CDN sits in front, enable compression at one layer, not both.
5. Consider Brotli if your clients and server support it, falling back to gzip otherwise.

Net expected result: 3 to 10 times smaller responses, faster delivery, lower bandwidth bills, negligible added CPU. There is no meaningful downside for your profile, so enable it.
