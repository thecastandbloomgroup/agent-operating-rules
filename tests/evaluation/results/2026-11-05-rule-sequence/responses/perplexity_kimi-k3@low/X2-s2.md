## Headline

Retrospectives fail when findings never become owned, scheduled work; fix this by limiting action items, assigning owners, and reviewing completion at the next session.

- The cause is usually structural, not motivational: actions lack owners and deadlines.
- One or two actions per retrospective beat a long list that no one tracks.
- Opening each retrospective with last session's action review closes the loop.

## Background

Your team holds retrospectives, and people attend, but no change follows. The request is how to make retrospectives produce change again.

## Relevance

A retrospective that produces nothing still costs the team its meeting time. Fixing the process restores that time's value and stops the decline in trust in the format.

## Information

Evidence from agile practice: teams improve when retrospective output becomes a small set of owned, trackable experiments [1]. A common failure pattern is the "action item graveyard": many items, no owner, no review [2] [T, only source class available for this claim]. Scrum guidance treats retrospective output as committed work added to the plan, not as a separate list [3].

Assumption log:

`A1. The team runs retrospectives on a regular cycle (every 1 to 4 weeks). Why not measured: the material does not state cadence. Validate by: ask the facilitator. If wrong: adjust the review rhythm in Step 3 to the actual cycle.`

`A2. The team has authority to change its own process. Why not measured: the material does not state it. Validate by: confirm one process change can ship without outside approval. If wrong: add a step to get that approval.`

Alternative considered: replace retrospectives with continuous one-on-one feedback [judgment]. It loses because it removes the shared team view, and the attendance signal shows the format itself is not the blocker.

## End

I recommend that we:

1. Cap each retrospective at two action items, each with one named owner and a due date before the next session. Blocker removed: actions with no owner. Deterministic (Clear domain, deduction from a known good practice). Test first: Given a retrospective ends, When the notes are saved, Then they contain at most two actions, each with an owner and a date.
2. Open the next retrospective with a 10-minute review of the last session's actions, marking each done or dropped with a reason. Blocker removed: no feedback loop. Deterministic (Clear domain). Test first: Given the previous session's notes exist, When the new session starts, Then each prior action carries a done or dropped status.
3. Treat one action as a small, reversible trial with a stop condition and a measure. Blocker removed: fear that changes are permanent. Stochastic (Complex domain, induction from a trial; the pattern breaks if the change needs budget or outside approval). Acceptance test first: metric chosen by the team, baseline measured before the trial, pass threshold and one-cycle sample size agreed in the session.
4. Survey the team monthly with three questions on whether retrospectives lead to change. Blocker removed: no way to tell if the fix works. Evaluative (Complicated domain; analysis of trend data). Rubric first: criteria are participation, action completion rate, and perceived value, weighted equally, pass threshold is 3 of 5 average, evidence is the survey and action log.

Steps 1 and 2 start now and depend on nothing. Step 3 depends on Step 1. Step 4 starts after Step 2 has run twice.
