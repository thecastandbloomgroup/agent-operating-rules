# Headline

Retrospective s fail because action items are not owned, tracked, or reviewed, so the fix is to close the loop. Three supporting points follow.

1. Limit each retrospective to one improvement item with a named owner and a due date.
2. Open every retrospective by reviewing the item from the last one.
3. Run a short diagnostic to learn why items stall today.

## Background

Your team holds retrospectives and people attend, but no change follows. You ask what to do. This pattern is common: the meeting produces talk, not commitments [judgment].

## Relevance

A retrospective that produces no change still costs the team one to two hours per sprint [judgment]. Fixing the follow-through recovers the value of that time.

## Information

### Evidence

- The Scrum Guide says the Sprint Retrospective exists to plan ways to increase quality and effectiveness, and that improvements "may be added to the Sprint Backlog" [1]. Attendance without added improvements misses that purpose.
- Derby and Larsen describe a five-stage retrospective that ends in "Close the Retrospective," where the team commits to specific actions with owners [2]. Their model has informed industry practice since 2006.
- Theme from practice [judgment]: action items die when they have no owner, no due date, and no review in the next session. This view rests on judgment, not on a measure from your team, so Step 3 below tests it.

### Assumption log

A1. Your retrospectives produce action items, but nobody tracks them afterward. Why not measured: the material does not state it. Validate by: check the notes of the last three retrospectives for owner and status fields. If wrong: run the diagnostic in Step 3 first and reorder the rest.

### Alternative considered

The strongest alternative is to replace retrospectives with ad hoc problem talks. It loses because the problem is follow-through, not the meeting format, and removing the ritual removes the last place where change is planned [judgment].

## End

I recommend that we take these three steps.

1. **Cap the output at one item per retrospective.** Write the item with a named owner and a due date, then put it on the sprint board.
   - Blocker removed: items without owners die [judgment].
   - Kind and domain: deterministic, Clear. Cause and effect is known, per the known rule in [2]; this is deduction.
   - Test (write it first):
     - Given a finished retrospective.
     - When the facilitator records the outcome.
     - Then exactly one item exists on the board, with one owner and one due date.
2. **Open the next retrospective with a five-minute review of the last item.**
   - Blocker removed: no closing of the loop.
   - Kind and domain: deterministic, Clear. Deduction from the same known rule [2].
   - Test (write it first):
     - Given the agenda for the next retrospective.
     - When the meeting starts.
     - Then the last item is marked done, dropped, or carried, with the reason recorded.
3. **Run a one-session diagnostic on why items stall.** This step MUST NOT start until Step 2 has run twice.
   - Blocker removed: unknown root cause, in case A1 is wrong.
   - Kind and domain: evaluative, Complex. Cause and effect is only visible after the probe, so this is induction. The pattern breaks if attendance itself is the cause.
   - Rubric (write it first), criteria with weights: item too large (30%), no owner power to change it (30%), no time in sprint (20%), low team interest (20%). Pass threshold: one criterion scores above 40%. Evidence: a five-question anonymous survey, all team members respond. Sample size: one session, full team.

## Follow-up

No questions. The recommendation changes if A1 proves false: in that case, run Step 3 first. Do not change the format of the meeting before follow-through is tested.
