## Headline

Repair the loop around the retrospective, not the meeting: close old actions first, cap new ones at two with owners, and probe for hidden causes.

- Follow-through is the first suspect: nothing closes old actions, so new ones die the same way [judgment].
- Action design is the second suspect: too many items, no owner, or work too big for one iteration [judgment].
- Input is the third suspect: the room works on symptoms, or fear keeps the real problems unspoken [judgment].

## Background

Your team holds retrospectives, the regular meeting where a team reviews its own way of working [1]. People attend, but the sessions produce no visible change. You ask what to do.

## Relevance

A meeting that changes nothing still consumes the hours. In addition, each quiet cycle teaches the team that raising problems has no effect, so honesty falls [judgment].

## Information

This section covers the evidence, the assumptions, and the alternative.

**Evidence.** The Scrum Guide gives the retrospective one purpose: plan ways to improve effectiveness [1]. Derby and Larsen describe the standard repair: few action items, each with an owner, each reviewed at the next session [2]. Kerth adds that people name real problems only when the room feels safe [3]. Each step below names a Cynefin domain, a label for how knowable cause and effect are in that work [4].

**Diagnosis.** The pattern "attends, nothing shifts" is 70% likely a broken closure loop [judgment]. Basis: attendance shows input exists, so the failure sits after the meeting. The three suspects split the path from problem to change into three non-overlapping stages: what enters the meeting, what leaves it, and what happens after it [judgment].

**Assumptions.**

A1. Retrospectives run on a regular cadence, and a facilitator leads each session. Why not measured: the material does not state it. Validate by: check the calendar and the notes of the last two sessions. If wrong: fix cadence and facilitation first; the four steps then apply unchanged.

A2. Actions die from missing follow-through, not because management overturns them. Why not measured: the material does not say what happens to actions after the meeting. Validate by: Step 1 shows each action's status in one session. If wrong: replace Step 3 with a decision-rights agreement with the manager.

A3. People speak honestly in the room. Why not measured: the material does not state it. Validate by: the Step 4 pulse. If wrong: shift effort to safety work before root-cause work [3].

**Alternative considered.** The strongest alternative is a format change: new templates or an outside facilitator. It loses because format changes what happens inside the meeting, and the failure sits after it [judgment]. An outside facilitator also leaves when the meeting ends, so the closure loop stays broken.

## End

I recommend that we repair the closure loop in four steps, in this order. Step 1 comes first because no new action survives without closure [judgment].

1. Kind: deterministic. Test, written first:
Given the action list from the previous session,
When the session opens,
Then each action is marked done, dropped with a stated reason, or carried once with a new date.
Then do: open the next session with a ten-minute review of every open action. Blocker removed: old actions vanish without closure. Domain: Clear; deduction from a documented best practice [2].

2. Kind: deterministic. Test, written first:
Given the session is closing,
When the team writes its actions,
Then each action has one owner, starts with a verb, and has a done date inside the current iteration.
Then do: close the same session with at most two actions, after Step 1 has passed. Blocker removed: vague, unowned, oversized items. Domain: Clear; deduction from a documented best practice [2].

3. Kind: deterministic. This step MUST NOT start until Step 2 has passed once. Test, written first:
Given the most frequent complaint of the last three sessions,
When the team records a chain of five why-questions,
Then the final cause sits inside the team's control, and one new action targets it.
Then do: run one five-whys pass on that complaint. Five whys is a method that asks why in sequence to reach a root cause. Blocker removed: actions that treat symptoms. Domain: Complicated; the cause is knowable through analysis, and the five-whys chain is that analysis [2].

4. Kind: stochastic. Run this probe in parallel with Steps 1 to 3. Budget: two sessions, fifteen minutes per session. Acceptance test, written first. Metric: share of scores of 4 or 5. Baseline: none; the first run sets it. Threshold: below 60%, treat safety as the main blocker. Sample size: all attendees across two sessions. Stop condition: after two sessions, read the result and decide. Then do: add one anonymous question to the next two sessions. The question reads: "On a scale of 1 to 5, can you raise real problems in this meeting?" Blocker removed: the untested guess in A3. Domain: Complex; the cause of silence shows only afterward, so this is a safe-to-fail probe, and its leverage is a guess. Pattern break to watch: high scores plus continued silence, which points to A2, not fear [3].

## Follow-up

Conditions that change the recommendation. If A2 proves false, replace Step 3 with a decision-rights agreement with the manager. If the Step 4 pulse falls below 60%, shift Step 3 to safety work: anonymous formats and written working agreements [3].
